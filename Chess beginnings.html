The six pieces

Each side starts with sixteen pieces. Values are a rough guide to how much each is worth when you trade.

♚

King

Moves one square in any direction. It can never move into check, and losing it means losing the game.

Value: priceless
♛

Queen

Moves any distance along a rank, file, or diagonal. The strongest piece on the board.

Value: 9 points
♜

Rook

Moves any distance in a straight line, forward, back, or sideways. Strongest on open files.

Value: 5 points
♝

Bishop

Moves any distance diagonally and stays on the same color of square for the whole game.

Value: 3 points
♞

Knight

Jumps in an L: two squares one way, then one to the side. It is the only piece that can leap over others.

Value: 3 points
♟︎

Pawn

Moves forward one square, or two from its starting square. It captures one square diagonally forward and promotes when it reaches the far side.

Value: 1 point

Castling

The king moves two squares toward a rook, and that rook jumps to the other side of the king. It protects your king and activates the rook.

En passant

If an enemy pawn advances two squares past your pawn, you may capture it as if it had moved only one, but only on the very next move.

Promotion

A pawn that reaches the last rank becomes a queen, rook, bishop, or knight of your choice. Most players choose a queen.

Ways to give check

Almost every checkmate starts with a check. Knowing the different kinds helps you attack, and helps you spot danger before it lands.

Direct check

The piece you just moved attacks the king. It is the simplest kind, and the opponent must answer it at once.

Discovered check

You move one piece out of the way, and a piece behind it attacks the king. The piece that moved can make its own threat at the same time, so the opponent faces two problems.

Double check

Two pieces attack the king at once, often when a discovered check is also a direct check. You cannot capture or block both, so the king must move.

Knight and pawn checks

These cannot be blocked, because a knight jumps and a pawn attacks from an adjacent square. The answer is to capture the checker or move the king.

Perpetual check

You keep giving check and the opponent cannot escape the sequence. The game ends in a draw, which is a useful rescue when you are behind.

Answering a check

You have three options: capture the attacker, block the line of attack, or move the king. If none is possible, it is checkmate.

A habit worth building: before every move, ask which checks your opponent could give next, and which checks you could give.

Check and checkmate

Check means the king is under attack. You must respond by capturing the attacker, blocking it, or moving the king. Checkmate means check with no legal way out, and the game ends right there.

Mate in two

White to move. Find a first move that forces checkmate on your next turn, whatever Black does. Click a piece, then its destination. Black replies automatically, and then you deliver the mate.

Other ways a game ends

Stalemate

The player to move is not in check but has no legal move. The game is a draw, not a win.

Resignation

A player concedes when the position is lost. This is common at every level.

Draw

By agreement, by repeating the same position three times, by insufficient material, or after fifty moves without a capture or pawn move.
